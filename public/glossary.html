Glossary

Reference

MeterLab’s stats are quick “signals” about a track. They’re best used for comparison and decision-making (e.g., matching a playlist, checking mix balance), not as absolute truth.

Musical

Key

What it is: The most likely tonic (C, C#, D…).

Why it matters: Helpful for harmonic mixing, mashups, and choosing compatible samples.

Genre notes: EDM/house/DJ sets often benefit from key-matching; jazz and some live recordings may modulate and confuse single-key estimation.

Scale (Major/Minor + Modes)

What it is: A best-fit scale quality (major/minor) and common modes (dorian, phrygian, lydian, mixolydian, locrian).

Why it matters: Gives a hint of “mood” (bright vs dark) and common chord vocabulary.

Genre notes: Rock/blues often leans mixolydian; metal can lean phrygian; funk can show dorian; pop is frequently major/minor.

Confidence

What it is: How strongly the chroma profile matches the best-fitting key/scale model.

Why it matters: Low confidence usually means: weak tonality, lots of noise/percussion, heavy modulation, or sparse harmonic content.

Genre notes: Ambient/drone and very percussive tracks often produce lower confidence.

Tempo

BPM

What it is: Estimated tempo (beats per minute) from onset periodicity (transient pulses).

Why it matters: Useful for beatmatching, planning transitions, and picking time-synced effects.

Genre notes: EDM/house/techno typically yields strong BPM confidence; classical, rubato, and ambient may not have a stable tempo.

Spectral / Tone

Brightness (Spectral Centroid)

What it is: A “center of mass” of frequency energy — higher means more high-end content.

Why it matters: Quick check for tonal tilt: dull/warm vs bright/present.

Genre notes: Pop often trends brighter; lo-fi and some hip-hop can be intentionally warmer; harsh brightness can fatigue in metal/EDM if overdone.

Roll-off

What it is: A frequency below which most spectral energy sits (here ~85%).

Why it matters: Helps spot “air” vs “mid-heavy” mixes and the presence/absence of high-frequency content.

Genre notes: Modern pop/EDM often has higher roll-off (more extended highs); older recordings or vinyl rips can roll off earlier.

Flatness

What it is: How noise-like the spectrum is — low = more harmonic/tonal, high = noisier.

Why it matters: Can hint at distorted/noisy textures vs clean harmonic content.

Genre notes: Distorted guitars, aggressive synths, and noisy percussion can raise flatness; acoustic/classical tends to be more harmonic.

Zero Crossings (ZCR)

What it is: How often the waveform crosses zero — loosely associated with noisiness/brightness and transient density.

Why it matters: Quick proxy for “busy/noisy” vs “smooth/tonal” signals.

Genre notes: Hi-hats/noise layers can push ZCR up; bass-heavy, sustained material tends to lower it.

Dynamics / Level

Loudness (LUFS)

What it is: A loudness estimate aligned to perceived level (roughly comparable across tracks).

Why it matters: Helps match levels between songs and detect overly hot masters.

Genre notes: EDM/modern pop often targets louder masters; classical and jazz usually preserve more headroom.

Average RMS & Peak Level

What it is: RMS is average power; peak is the highest sample magnitude (both expressed in dBFS here).

Why it matters: RMS reflects “density”; peaks reflect headroom and transient sharpness.

Genre notes: Hip-hop can have high low-end RMS; acoustic can show lower RMS but higher transient peaks (snare, plucks).

Crest Factor

What it is: Peak-to-average ratio (in dB). Higher crest = more dynamic range/punch.

Why it matters: Quick view of compression/limiting intensity and transient impact.

Genre notes: Modern EDM/pop often has lower crest (heavier limiting); live recordings/classical often have higher crest.

Duration (and “partial”)

What it is: Duration is the analyzed window length; “partial” means only the first ~2 minutes were analyzed for performance.

Why it matters: If a song changes dramatically later, the stats reflect the analyzed segment.
